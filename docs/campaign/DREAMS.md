# Dreams — the writing worksheet

> **STATUS: TEMPLATE / AUTHOR WORKSHEET / EXAMPLE ONLY — NOT CANON.** Issue
> [#1152](https://github.com/TomiToivio/NooPunk/issues/1152), Phase 1.
> **Tomi owns every dream, image, line of dialogue and interpretation.** Everything below that is not
> labelled is scaffolding written to show the *shape* of an answer, and it is disposable.

**This is the single canonical home for dream-writing templates.** If another document starts
describing dream scenes, it is a duplicate: extend this one, or reduce it to a pointer.

**What this file is not:** a rules engine, a second astral system, or a decoder. The mechanics live in
[the psionics draft](../design/DRAFT_NOOPUNK_PSIONICS.md); dreams live here. Nothing in this file
grants a character a power.

---

## 1. The one rule

**A dream's provenance is ambiguous, and the game never labels it.**

A dream can be private, symbolic, shared, nonphysical, precognitive, telepathic, or nothing
supernatural at all. Several can be true at once. The narration shows what the character
experiences; it does not say where it came from.

This is not coyness for its own sake. The moment a dream is stamped "astral projection", the player
stops interpreting and starts reading a map. Interpretation **is** the gameplay.

**What follows from the rule:**

- No narration may say "this was a vision", "this was telepathy", or "this was only a dream".
- A dream that later turns out to have been paranormal must still have read as ambiguous when it
  happened.
- A dream that was purely psychological must still have been capable of frightening the character.
- The GM may know more than the player. The player-facing text does not.

## 2. What a dream can be made of

Write these as **overlapping possibilities**, never as a list the player is meant to pick from:

- **Individual subconscious** — memory, desire, anxiety, trauma, free association, invention.
- **Collective unconscious** — archetypes, shared cultural imagery, motifs that recur across people.
- **Astral / Noöspace** — spontaneous or involuntary projection, apparent encounters in nonphysical
  places.
- **Time/Space** — dream geography where chronology, distance and cause do not behave like waking life.
  This is not a subway map and it is not time travel.
- **Telepathic contact** — a message, or bleed-through from a mind that did not mean to send.
- **Precognition** — a possible future, symbolic, partial, wrong as often as right.
- **Mixed and unresolved** — several at once, unreliable memory, deception, or nothing at all.

**Tomi writes here:** which of these actually operate in his campaign, and which are red herrings
characters believe in.

## 3. No PSI prerequisite

**A character with no deliberate psychic ability whatsoever can have an involuntary astral or
psychic dream.** That is a premise of the setting, not a reward for a build choice.

What a dream **never** does:

- grant daytime telepathy, voluntary projection, clairvoyance or any new ability
- prove that the character is psychic
- produce evidence that stands up in an investigation
- solve a case, a relationship or a mystery

What a dream **can** do: frighten, comfort, unsettle, hint, rhyme with waking events, and make the
character (and the player) wonder.

**Tomi writes here:** what his protagonist believes about her own mind at each point in the campaign.

## 4. Dream Helsinki

The protagonist may dream of a Helsinki she recognises and that cannot be correct: **neighbourhoods
and buildings from different centuries standing next to each other**. Nineteenth-century streets
beside twentieth-century university corridors beside districts that do not exist yet.

**The University beneath the city.** A dream version of the University of Helsinki may contain a vast
labyrinth of tunnels, lecture halls and stairwells that are not in any physical survey. This is dream
geography — an astral or oneiric representation — **not a claim that real tunnels are accessible**.
A dream is not a maintenance map, and an investigator cannot walk into a sealed real corridor because
she dreamt about it.

Rules for writing it:

- A dream route may be familiar, may change between episodes, and may be reliable in one dream and
  wrong in the next.
- Physical, Cyber and Astral are distinct presentations of a place. Time/Space is a fourth thing, and
  it is not "the astral tube map".
- When a dream resembles a waking location, that resemblance licenses **investigation**, never
  confirmation.
- Invented 2045 tunnels are explicitly fictional and must not be pinned to a real restricted site.

**Tomi writes here:** his dream Helsinki — which real places appear, which impossible ones, what the
recurring landmarks are, and what each one means to him (if anything).

## 5. How dreams enter play

**Sleep is the trigger, not the plot.** Not every night needs a revelation; most should not have one.

A useful mix, deliberately uneven:

- **Mundane** — a memory, a worry, a conversation that already happened, slightly wrong.
- **Comic** — the institution dream: forms that cannot be filed, a colleague who is also a corridor.
- **Tender** — a person the character misses, in a place that never existed.
- **Disturbing** — the one that makes the player put the controller down.
- **Uncanny** — nothing happens, and it is the wrongness that stays.

**Length:** a vignette of three or four images is often stronger than a navigable scene. Reserve the
long, explorable dreams for the moments that earn them.

**Tomi writes here:** how often dreams occur, whether the player chooses to sleep, and whether the
dream can be interrupted.

## 6. The scene format

Seven parts. Write them in plain prose; the headings are a checklist, not a form.

1. **Title.** What the player sees in a journal afterwards, if anything.
2. **When it can occur.** The trigger in story terms — after a shift, after a specific conversation,
   after a bad night — and whether it recurs.
3. **What the protagonist experiences.** The dream itself: places, images, people, the order things
   happen in. This is the writing.
4. **What the player can do.** Move, look, follow, speak, refuse, remember a detail, wake up. Say
   which choices matter and which are texture.
5. **What remains unclear.** The competing explanations, stated as questions rather than answers.
6. **Later callbacks.** An ordinary waking detail that echoes the dream without proving it.
7. **GM-only interpretations.** What may actually be going on, for the GM's eyes. Optional, revisable,
   and **never** shown to the player.

Mark anything the players must not know as **GM SECRET**.

## 7. Two worked examples

> **EXAMPLE ONLY — NOT CANON.** These exist to show the format. Replace them entirely.

### *The University Beneath*

**When it can occur.** After an ordinary first-week shift. No conscious PSI needed.

**What the protagonist experiences.** A familiar staircase — the one in the building where she files
reports — keeps going down past the floor where it should stop. Below it: a chain of lecture halls
that are too large for the building, lit like a 1970s corridor, with rain audible somewhere. One door
opens onto nineteenth-century Helsinki in summer. Another opens onto a street she half-recognises as
her own, rebuilt. A person whose face she almost knows is looking for a lost classroom and is
irritated, not frightened, that it has moved again.

**What the player can do.** Follow the person, open a door, sit in a lecture hall and listen, look for
the rain, or wake up.

**What remains unclear.** Memory collage, collective imagery, an astral place, Time/Space, someone
else's dream, or a story she was told as a child and has forgotten.

**Later callbacks.** A real corridor that shares the staircase's proportions. A colleague who mentions
a lecture hall that was demolished in 1998.

**GM-only interpretations.** *GM SECRET — Tomi writes this.*

### *The Form That Cannot Be Filed*

**When it can occur.** During a week of paperwork, especially after a case is closed over her
objection.

**What the protagonist experiences.** She is at her desk. The form has a field she cannot read and
cannot leave blank. Each time she looks away, one more field has appeared. A colleague — helpful,
cheerful, entirely at ease — explains that she must simply submit it correctly, and does not say how.
The queue behind her is patient. Somewhere in the building a lift arrives, over and over, and nobody
gets out.

**What the player can do.** Fill the form, ask the colleague, leave the desk, look at the queue, wake
up.

**What remains unclear.** Anxiety, an institutional archetype, a real memory of a real form, or
something that noticed her.

**Later callbacks.** A real form with an unreadable field. A lift that does the same thing in waking
life.

**GM-only interpretations.** *GM SECRET — Tomi writes this.*

## 8. Continuity

Dreams belong to the same character as the case files. They can refer to:

- people she knows, and how those relationships are actually going
- cases, including unresolved ones
- choices she made and regrets
- motifs that recur across dreams, growing or changing
- the act she is in — and therefore what she is allowed to suspect

**Dreams must not contradict authored chronology**, and must not reveal late truths early.

**Tomi writes here:** the recurring motifs, the symbols he wants to seed, and where each one is
allowed to pay off.

## 9. Limits: precognition, telepathy, investigation

Dreams are **suggestive, not admissible**.

- No guaranteed prophecy. A dream can be wrong, partial, or about the wrong person.
- No unlimited remote viewing and no unconsented interrogation.
- No dream produces evidence that stands in front of a prosecutor.
- A dream never solves a case. It can send the character looking in a direction she would not have
  tried.

The authority on what psychic ability may do in an investigation is
[the PSI/investigation boundary note](../rules/PSI_INVESTIGATION_BOUNDARY_1105.md): **a lead, never a
proof**.

**Tomi writes here:** how much a dream is allowed to help, and what it must never give away.

## 10. Dream actions and waking consequences

Inside a dream she may move, speak, explore, meet people, and sometimes appear to use abilities she
does not have awake. Those moments are **scene-bound**: they do not unlock anything.

**What persists:** reactions, journal fragments, motifs, emotional state, new suspicions, later
callbacks, and what she chooses to tell people.

**What does not persist without an approved rule or a scripted story consequence:** physical injury,
exact information, mechanical advancement, or any objective change in the waking world.

**Tomi writes here:** whether a dream can ever leave a mark — and if so, how rare and how costly.

## 11. Spoiler discipline

| Act | Dreams may | Dreams must not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **I** | Foreshadow, unsettle, rhyme, repeat a motif | Confirm anything supernatural, name the mystery, show the Wanderer |
| **II** | Show the awakening from inside, badly understood | Explain what the awakening *was* |
| **III** | Let astral geography become navigable | Hand over the answer to the conspiracy |
| **IV** | Show the Higher Self, the choice before incarnation | Reveal the specific service before the ending |

**Tomi writes here:** his own spoiler boundaries, act by act.

## 12. Unresolved questions for the author

1. Does the player choose to sleep, or do dreams arrive on a schedule?
2. Can a dream scene be failed? If so, what does failure cost, and does it lock content?
3. Which motifs recur, and how many times before they pay off?
4. Is Dream Helsinki the same city each time, or does it drift?
5. Does the protagonist ever learn what any of it was — and if so, how wrong is she allowed to be?
6. How do dreams interact with the campaign's memory/revelation plan
   ([campaign outline §5](CAMPAIGN_01_EUROPOL_ROOKIE.md)) — are they a carrier, or a parallel channel?
