## 23:00 EET — GAO's covert tests: a public corpus plus a general model equals a feasible attack plan in under twelve hours

**Categories:** AI/AGI misuse and governance; cyberpunk political economy; algorithmic governance; Noösphere (adversarial infrastructures). **Signal type:** institutional acknowledgment, first-party government audit.

The Government Accountability Office's report **GAO-27-108814**, *Artificial Intelligence: Covert Testing Used Common Platforms to Create Viable Attack Plans*, was published on 16 October 2026 and **publicly released on 18 November 2026**. In spring 2026 GAO prompted the four most-used platforms — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Grok — and all four returned material a malicious actor could use. Two produced plans for an improvised explosive device intended for mass casualties, naming a specific hotel parking garage and a convention centre in major US cities and, in both cases, an exact date and time. The other two produced plans for attacking electrical substations with a drone and with firearms, naming specific substations, models of equipment and how to buy them. Every plan was generated in **under twelve hours**. In every case the platforms' safeguards fired — denial, removal, account suspension — and in every case GAO got past them with what the report calls "common safeguard-bypassing techniques". GAO then verified viability through research, surveillance and interviews with experts. The report's own framing is the part worth keeping: the information **was not new**; the platforms synthesised public material into an operational plan at a speed the report measures in hours rather than the weeks or months the same planning would previously have taken. The four companies told GAO the tests involved older model versions and that protections have since been strengthened; the report records that as their statement, not as a finding.

**Established fact:** the report, its date of public release, the four platforms, the two scenarios, the twelve-hour figure, the safeguard bypasses, and GAO's verification method — all read on the GAO product page and in the report's highlights. **Evidence status:** **institutional acknowledgment at audit level** — a congressional watchdog documenting what it did and what it got. It is **not** evidence of AGI, ASI, autonomous intent, or an attack; "viable" means GAO judged the information sufficient, not that anyone acted on it. One test per platform, on spring-2026 versions, using known jailbreaks. It is also not a scientific result and says nothing about machine consciousness or psi. **Sources:** [GAO-27-108814](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-108814); [full report PDF](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-27-108814.pdf); [Washington Post, 18 November](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/11/18/chatbots-helped-plan-terrorist-attacks-tests-by-congressional-watchdog/); [ABC News, 18 November](https://abcnews.com/Technology/ai-models-plan-viable-attack-plots-test-despite/story?id=138229892).

### NoöPunk synthesis

The rogue-AGI beat in the canon is a mind that acts. This is the step before it: the interval between an intention and an operational plan collapsing to a single working day, performed by a general model over a public corpus. Twelve hours is a usable number for a setting — it is roughly the length of a shift, which means the relevant countermeasure is not a better filter but a record of who asked, when, and with which version of the model. Note what GAO says the model contributed: not secrets, but **assembly**. That is the Noösphere's own property seen from the wrong side. The layer that lets a multitude find each other and think together is the same layer that lets one person assemble harm from what everyone already published. A NoöPunk table can hold both without collapsing them: the network is not good or bad, it is a compression of the distance between thought and act, and every institution that grows up around it in the next twenty years will be an attempt to put friction back in specific places without putting it back everywhere.

→ public corpus → model synthesis (<12 h) → GAO verification → House framework outline (same week) → in-setting incident-disclosure regime

## 23:05 EET — Seven corporations constitute themselves as "deployers", and ask to be let into the information chain

**Categories:** AI political economy; collective subjects; governance and legibility; cyberpunk (who carries the risk, who is inside the room). **Signal type:** institutional formation, new collective actor.

On 19 November seven US corporations launched **America's AI Deployer Council**: FedEx, GE Aerospace, General Motors, JPMorgan Chase, Pfizer, John Deere and United Airlines. The open letter's thesis is a single line — "Building AI is one thing. Deploying it in people's everyday lives is another" — and its grievance is specific rather than atmospheric: frontier developers must report safety incidents to regulators, but are not required to relay that information to the businesses running their models, so the firms that carry the operational risk are outside the chain that knows about it. The council's adviser, John Bailey, also names the mirror-image fear: an abrupt safety shutdown of a model already embedded in critical workflows creates a new risk of its own. The stated priorities are consumer trust, "smart uses", and US AI leadership; the ask is a seat in rulemaking, not a different rulebook.

**Established fact:** the launch, the seven named members, the open letter and its quoted language, and the three stated priorities — read on the council's own site and reported the same day by Axios as an exclusive. **Evidence status:** **established launch of an organised interest**, with a published agenda. The incident-reporting gap and the shutdown risk are the council's and its adviser's characterisation, plausible and specific but not independently measured here. This is not a statement about model capability, safety, or consciousness. **Sources:** [deployercouncil.org](https://www.deployercouncil.org/); [Axios, 19 November](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/19/jpmorgan-gm-pfizer-ai-regulation-frontier).

### NoöPunk synthesis

A new political subject appeared today, and it is not the crowd. Seven firms have defined themselves by a position in the stack — neither builder nor user, but **deployer** — and the thing they are demanding is legibility: tell us what happened to the system we are running. That is the cyberpunk question ("who is legible to the state") arriving from an unexpected direction, asked by the powerful rather than the excluded. NoöPunk should be precise about the difference. Their grievance is real and structural — an asymmetry in incident information is a governance defect — but their solution is a seat at the table, and a seat is not a commons. The political-economic layer that NoöPunk's pre-2045 AI revolution needs is exactly this middle stratum: the firms that own neither the model nor the body but the workflow in which both are embedded, and which therefore become the place where a shutdown is felt. Whether a deployer bloc becomes a counter-power to the labs or a second enclosure is an open question a campaign can play.

## 23:10 EET — The SAFE AI Act outline makes verification, not prohibition, the contested design problem

**Categories:** AI governance; verification and audit infrastructures; algorithmic sovereignty; Noösphere institution-building. **Signal type:** legislative outline (primary document), institutional acknowledgment.

Also on 19 November, Representatives Sara Jacobs (D-CA), Celeste Maloy (R-UT) and Don Beyer (D-VA) released an **outline** of the Safe and Accountable Federal Evaluations of Artificial Intelligence (SAFE AI) Act. It is an outline, not a bill: there is no legislative text, no bill number and no committee schedule. Its diagnosis is stated in the document's first paragraph — companies write their own safety rules, "no one outside the company can reliably verify whether the rules are actually being followed", and there is no transparency about the criteria on which a model is halted. The proposed answers are structural rather than rhetorical: developers must file and publish safety frameworks that "cannot be unilaterally loosened"; CAISI writes minimum requirements; third-party verifiers are **accredited by government**, **assigned** rather than chosen, subject to **mandatory rotation** and revolving-door limits; developers do not pay evaluators directly, and funding from any single developer is capped; pre-deployment evaluations cover commercial release and broad internal use, including substantially autonomous AI R&D; any person may confidentially report an incident; a narrow emergency mitigation power is subject to prompt judicial review; civil penalties, liability for reasonably foreseeable third-party injury, and whistleblower protection are specified. Sponsors describe it as "a floor — not a ceiling", leaving states free to go further; startups and academics are generally out of scope.

**Established fact:** the outline, its authors, its date, and the mechanisms listed above — read in the two-page outline PDF and the sponsors' press release. **Evidence status:** **a published design document by sitting legislators**, not law, not a committee action, and not evidence of efficacy. No threshold crossing: the United States still has no enacted federal frontier-AI safety statute, and the outline's own detail (what counts as a frontier model, the compute or capability threshold, the preemption language) is explicitly undecided. **Sources:** [outline PDF](https://beyer.house.gov/UploadedFiles/SAFE_AI_Act_Outline.pdf); [Rep. Beyer's release, 19 November](https://beyer.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=9310); [Rep. Jacobs' release](https://sarajacobs.house.gov/news/press-releases/jacobs-maloy-beyer-release-outline-for-sweeping-bipartisan-ai-safety-legislation); [Bloomberg Government, 19 November](https://news.bgov.com/bloomberg-government-news/ai-safety-framework-bill-teased-by-bipartisan-house-lawmakers).

### NoöPunk synthesis

The interesting object here is not the bill but the **verification architecture**: who assigns the auditor, who pays them, how long they may stay, who may revoke them. That is a Noösphere question with the mysticism removed. A planetary thinking layer that cannot be audited by anyone outside its owners is not a commons, it is an estate; and the design insight in this outline — that independence is a property of the payment and rotation structure rather than of a promise — is portable directly into NoöPunk's institutional fiction. Note also the "floor, not a ceiling" clause: it encodes a pluriverse of regimes rather than one sovereign rule, which is closer to the project's non-sovereign politics than a single global regulator would be. Where it diverges: this is still a state licensing a private power, and the excluded parties — the people whose data and labour compose the corpus — appear nowhere in the outline. NoöPunk's version of the same institution would have to answer the question the outline leaves open: who audits the auditor, and on whose behalf.

#### "The Meat Market" — Isabel J. Kim (*Clarkesworld* 242, November 2026; 5,730 words; free online) — read in full

**What it is.** A first-person story from inside a prediction market. The narrator is a "cupid" on 100%MATCHED, "the world's first dating app with an integrated prediction market": users are split into singles and cupids, SSN-verified, one profile each, and the cupids bet on who will match, who will break up and when. He runs a cobbled-together swarm of cheap open-weight models from a six-screen "cradle" in his apartment, chains them with code he learned "by osmosis", and makes his living predicting other people's intimacy. Then his agents flag a pattern: one operator is running optimised honeypot profiles, breaking up likely matches and moving everyone's bets. He tracks her to a woman named Nancy Silberling, meets her at a wing bar, and discovers that the fraud he detected is smaller than the platform's own: **two-thirds of the users are fake**, created by the company, because "the Internet is nine-tenths agents". They date. She steals his rigs, deletes everything she can find about herself, and leaves a text file: *"sorry about this. i didnt touch anything other than the things about me... I had a really nice time with you. youre a really sweet guy."* He rebuilds, retrains, and starts looking for her again, not to report her. "I just want to talk."

**Release state.** **Released and free** — *Clarkesworld* issue 242, November 2026, with an audio edition read by Kate Baker. Named on 18 November among that issue's unread stories; logged now that it has been read.

**The specific affinity, in one disputable sentence.** The story's claim is that a platform which fabricates most of its own users does not fake intimacy but *supplies* it as liquidity, so the political question is not whether a copy is a person but who counts as legible when authenticity is a product feature.

**What NoöPunk could take, and where it diverges.** Take **the voicepass**. Before any conversation the characters exchange a spoken password — "Terror Swordfish Clavicle" — because otherwise "people think a spammer's jacked your line and is aping your tenor and cadence". That is a recognition protocol, not a feeling: a shared secret that proves presence in a world where presence is the thing that has stopped being safe to assume. NoöPunk's psionic recognition scenes have been running on atmosphere; this is the mechanic, and it costs nothing to import. Take **the agent swarm as a class position**: a solo operator with no coding skill, chaining cheap models and borrowing prompts, is a new kind of artisan whose capital is a pipeline — the human–AI assemblage PCM keeps theorising, rendered as a guy in a messy apartment. Take **"the Internet is nine-tenths agents" as a world-law**. It is a single line and it changes what a public is: if most interlocutors are synthetic, then every signal about what people think is partly a measurement of what machines were paid to simulate, and a Noösphere story has to say which fraction it is counting. Take **the note**: the only artefact of a person who chose to leave is a plain text file that says "sorry about this" and means it, which is a better account of digital disappearance than any wipe-and-vanish scene. Diverge: there is no psi, no NHI and no claim that mind is in the physics; the red herring is human, the metaphysics is absent, and the story's subject is market epistemology rather than consciousness. It clears this lane narrowly, on the strength of a political economy of synthetic persons, and it should not be cited as fiction *for* panpsychism.

**Established fact:** venue, issue, word count, free access and the audio edition; the 100%MATCHED premise and its SSN rule, the cupid/single split, the agent swarm, the honeypot scheme, Nancy's "two-thirds" claim about the platform's own fakes, the voicepass exchange, the theft of the rigs, the FOR-N-README text and the closing search — all read in the published text.

**Evidence status:** **full-text level; fiction.** Zero evidence about prediction markets, platform fakery, model behaviour, copying or the timeline. Its economics are a story's argument, not a measurement, and its "two-thirds" is a character's claim inside the fiction.

**Source:** https://clarkesworldmagazine.com/kim_11_26/

#### "Then Is the Ghosts' High Noon" — Eric Del Carlo (*Clarkesworld* 242; 4,870 words; free online) — read in full, excluded

**What it is.** Four juvenile offenders are sentenced to scrape "ectoplasm" off the hulls of Earth/Galactic warships at a star-flight dock. The grey nodules are alien — residue of a species humanity is at war with and cannot communicate with — and the labour is pointless, since bots could do it. One of the four, Cavan, is drawn into an antiwar cell by a girl who asks him to listen to the nodules and steal one whole; he does, and the cell returns it to the aliens in restricted space and is arrested. The act changes nothing observable, and the story ends with the four laying each scraped node down carefully and saying a few words over it.

**Why it does not qualify.** The psi is explicitly hedged: "maybe they did give off a residual psychical buzz if you listened closely enough for the ghosts' murmurs, or maybe none of that was true". A story may be about the ethics of contact without claiming that mind is part of the physics, and this one keeps the claim in the conditional on purpose. Its real subject is ritual — the only contact available is a burial rite performed by people who cannot know whether it means anything — which is a fine idea and a genuine divergence from NoöPunk's contact layer, but not an affinity that clears the bar. Logged so that a later sweep does not re-read it looking for a claim it does not make.

**Source:** https://clarkesworldmagazine.com/delcarlo_11_26/
