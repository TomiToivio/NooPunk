## New institutional signal — autonomous weapons governance reaches the UN review-conference decision point

**Event:** 16–20 November 2026, Geneva. **Categories:** AI/AGI governance; drone warfare; cyberpunk; human–machine agency; geopolitical transformation. **Evidence:** established international meeting and agenda, **not** an enacted treaty.

The Seventh Review Conference of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons convened 128 state parties to decide the mandate and future work on lethal autonomous weapon systems (LAWS). A government-experts group adopted elements for a possible instrument on 4 September, without deciding whether it would be binding. The UN and ICRC have called for binding prohibitions and restrictions. Reuters reported on 16 November that the US, Russia and Israel oppose a binding pact while several European states support negotiations with varying positions on legal form. As of this morning **no verified final decision or new treaty is claimed**: the conference is still scheduled through 20 November. This is a decision-point watch item, not a claim of successful regulation.

**Why new for this watch:** Unlike the previously recorded Ukrainian operational human–robot warfare and AI cyberattacks, this is a distinct multilateral attempt to define who may delegate lethal decisions to machines. Its political stakes lie in human control, accountability and the asymmetry between rapid autonomous deployment and slow international consensus. It also tests whether Europe can articulate a common position without implying European federalization.

**Primary sources:** [UN Geneva conference and mandate](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/meetings-events/events/observances-special-events/2026/11/seventh-review-conference-high); [UN explanation](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/about/ungeneva-explained/should-machines-make-decisions); [Reuters, 16 November](https://www.reuters.com/world/us-russia-oppose-talks-limiting-autonomous-weapons-2026-11-16/). **Uncertainty:** final adopted conference text and whether negotiations become binding. Revisit official documents after the conference closes; do not report a treaty until verified.

#### "The Exchange Service" — R.T. Ester (*Clarkesworld* 242, November 2026; 9,330-word novelette; free online; audio edition read by Kate Baker) — read in full

**What it is.** A novelette about human beings used as components of a temporary mind. Meka volunteers as a "channel" in the Exchange Service: he sleeps in stasis and is woken when ninety-nine others are, and the hundred of them are lowered into a pool where a "hyper-conductive" secretion dissolves and links a hundred human nervous systems into one entity, the chorus Vela-23. Their job is to receive starseer, a Merge visitor that comes through an aperture called the Breach, and answer it the only way the Service has found: in chord progressions. "mother moon sound" is a progression the Merge associate with loss and grief and that can last thousands of years; a "leaving sound" ends a visit; a dispute is settled by which chord the human chorus is willing to produce. Meka cannot hear the Merge directly and remembers nothing afterwards. His daughter Kira could hear: she was a listener, recommended for the service before she could recite the alphabet and joining at sixteen, and she vanished from a chorus years ago. He has been searching for her across exchanges ever since. Then she starts appearing to him — by the pool, at the door of his berth — and Odion, a channel who failed the listener assessment, eventually admits she sees her too. "Your hallucinations are no longer just yours," Odion says. "Anybody else in the pool with you could also be planning their own escapade with Kira."

**Release state.** Released and free online in the November 2026 issue, with an audio edition; the issue is also on sale in ebook and print.

**The specific affinity, in one disputable sentence.** The story claims that contact between kinds of mind is possible only as a group mind assembled for the purpose, and that the price of it is autobiographical time: a listener who remembers the Merge's history ages by the act of remembering, so diplomacy is paid for in the lifespan of the person who holds it.

**What NoöPunk could take, and where it diverges.** Three usable things, all mechanical.

- **The chorus is a person, not a party.** Vela-23 has a name, a voice, a shared memory pool and a negotiating position, and the story is exact about what each of the hundred gives up to constitute it. A psi rule that treats a link as a bonus to a roll misses this: the thing assembled has its own continuity, and the interesting question is what happens to it when the pool drains and the hundred wake up as a hundred again.
- **The propagation rule.** A shared mind makes private grief a shared hazard: one member's revenant becomes everyone's possible revenant, and the story treats the risk as contamination and concealment rather than madness. That is an ontological-shock mechanic NoöPunk can lift whole — better than a sanity check, because the question is not whether you believe it but what the group now has to agree is real.
- **Power paid in years.** Listeners age with every act of remembering — "It's the act of remembering that kills you" — so the strongest faculty in the setting is the one that consumes the user's remaining life fastest. A cost that is diegetic, irreversible and unequally distributed is worth more to a game than a mana pool.

Divergence: the psi is exogenous — alien conductive chemistry plus a Service that owns the stasis pods and the pool — not a property of matter; there is no Earth, no Noösphere and no political economy of augmentation here; and the Merge are persons with an estimated 6,613-year grievance against a "moonbreaker" who destroyed their hospice worlds, handled by an institution that has already decided a centuries-long search is not worth the effort and is still negotiating over whether to pay for a monument. The story's contact layer is bureaucratic cost-benefit with a hymn tune, which is a warning rather than a model.

**Established fact:** venue, issue, word count, free access and audio edition; the Breach, the Service's stasis-and-chorus method, the hundred-channel linkage, "mother moon sound" and the 6,613-year grievance, the listener-aging rule, Kira's disappearance from the Asis-10 chorus, Odion's admission that she sees Kira too, the two station outages and the inconclusive investigation, Meka's removal for a fever the station says it cannot treat, and the closing identification of Meka with starseer — all read in the published text.

**Evidence status:** **full-text level; fiction.** Zero evidence about psi, NHI, group minds, contact or the timeline. A chord progression is not a documented communication channel and a chorus is not a laboratory result; the institutional logic is the story's argument, not a finding.

**Source:** https://clarkesworldmagazine.com/ester_11_26/

#### "Trimmed From The Night" — Raahem Alvi (*Clarkesworld* 242, November 2026; 11,090-word novelette; free online) — read in full; clears the lane narrowly, on the body-economy clause

**What it is.** Afrah and Kamran collect corpses for hire in an interstellar war. Their ship is a morgue with a red coffin painted on the hull; their clients are families and corporations; their working tool is a location tagger that tells them which scattered remains on a battlefield were a named person. A mercenary's contract lists "corpse extraction and location tagging implants" among its benefits; a PMC's war is "tax deductible". One retrieval is worth more than the others: Commander Edward Horn, whose head must stay frozen because "Commander Horn is carrying valuable company assets inside of a bio implant in his brain." Kamran's summary is the story's thesis: "some bodies are worth more than living people. And ten thousand corpses can be equivalent to one." When the ship is stranded, the ice goes to Horn's head and the ambassador's daughter rots in her cooler. Afrah spends most of the story arguing that consciousness is "just a survival strategy" and a human is meat. The last section is her lover's: trying to claim her body before the unclaimed are burned, reading that she is "one of two bodies" in a nine-point headline, and concluding that "Afrah Begum was DD's universe".

**Release state.** Released and free online in the November 2026 issue.

**The specific affinity, in one disputable sentence.** The story claims that a body's worth is set by ownership and legibility rather than by personhood — an implant that outlives its owner can make one corpse outbid ten thousand dead colonists — and that the dead remain persons only while someone is able to file a claim on them.

**What NoöPunk could take, and where it diverges.** Take **the price list**. NoöPunk's factions and psi mechanics have no schedule of what a body, a memory or a corpse costs, and this story supplies the shape of one: insured, uninsured, asset-bearing, next of kin spread across galaxies, "unclaimed bodies get burned". A setting whose central claim is that mind runs deeper than matter still has to say what matter is worth, or its politics is decoration. Take also **the implant as estate**: corporate property inside a skull that turns a death into a logistics problem and a mourning into a shipping dispute. Divergence: there is no psi and no noösphere, the metaphysics is aggressively deflationary for most of its length, and the story's resolution contradicts that metaphysics — the woman who insisted a person is meat dies asking to be told the story of a first date. That reversal is either the point or a wobble; it is not evidence of anything, and the story should not be cited as fiction for panpsychism. It clears the lane on the fourth clause, who owns the body and who is legible, and not on the first.

**Established fact:** venue, issue, word count and free access; the corpse-collection business and its location-tagging implants, Horn's bio-implant asset and the instruction to keep his head cold, Kamran's two valuations of a body, the destruction of Nos, the stranded landing, and the closing claim by DD — all read in the published text.

**Evidence status:** **full-text level; fiction.** Nothing here documents real corpse logistics, real implant ownership, real war economies or real mourning practice. Its figures are a character's arithmetic inside a story.

**Source:** https://clarkesworldmagazine.com/alvi_11_26/

#### Correction: *City of Espers* was not live on 19 November

Yesterday's entry described *City of Espers* (Nathan Blades / Three Sails Studios) as having "moved from the pre-launch page recorded on 18 November to a live campaign in its final week". Both halves are wrong, and the error is ours rather than the storefront's. The BackerKit project page states **"Project Ended — Ended on Sep 29, 2026"**, with £25,539 raised against an £8,000 goal from 598 backers, late pledges and preorders open, and shipping estimated for January 2027. The updates list "The campaign ends. The party continues!" (21 days ago), "Pledge Manager Smoke Test + Preorders Open" (8 days) and "Backerkit Surveys Now Available!" (7 days); *TTRPG Insider*'s September interview gives the same closing date, 29 September. The page was a post-campaign late-pledge page on both 18 and 19 November — not a pre-launch page and not a live campaign. The quoted tiers (£10 low income, £15 digital, £35 physical, £55 all-in) and the "written, edited, laid out, artwork outstanding" claim are accurate; the campaign state was not. The judgement is unchanged and now better founded: **screened, not judged**, because no quickstart, SRD or card-mechanic preview has been published, and a game that closed seven weeks ago and is in pledge management has no campaign momentum to mistake for quality.

**Sources:** [BackerKit project page](https://www.backerkit.com/c/projects/three-sails-studios/city-of-espers-a-psychic-powered-urban-action-ttrpg); [TTRPG Insider, 22 September](https://www.ttrpginsider.news/p/fight-for-your-turf-with-psychic-style-in-city-of-espers).
