## 23:10 EEST — The pre-Sputnik plates: an anomaly programme with a replication, a peer-reviewed validation and a credentialed rebuttal, all pointing at the same archive

On 7 October NewsNation's *Reality Check* (Ross Coulthart) ran an hour with **Beatriz Villarroel** (VASCO; Nordita, Stockholm University) and **Stephen Bruehl** (Vanderbilt) on the hotspot mapping of Palomar Observatory Sky Survey-I transients: star-like point sources recorded on photographic plates between November 1949 and April 1957 that are absent from every later survey — that is, from the sky *before* Sputnik, before any artificial satellite existed. Projected back to Earth under the assumption that the sources sit in geosynchronous orbit, the high-probability transients do not scatter at random: they cluster over the Pacific west of southern Mexico and Central America, the southern Gulf of Mexico near Tampico, and the south-western United States (Sedona, White Sands). The nuclear-test association separates by location — Pacific transients on Pacific test dates, south-western US transients on Nevada test dates — and a reported 203 high-probability transients appear over the Pacific on the night before the Castle Yankee detonation in May 1954. The episode also relays independent work by the former Hubble scientist Ivo Busko on plates from a German observatory, reported to show the same date-spike on 4 April 1956. By the end, Villarroel described a possible surveillance system, Bruehl put the odds of non-human monitoring at better than 50 percent, and both said three-letter agencies have taken notice.

This is not a podcast-only claim, and its public record includes a real disagreement:

- **Peer-reviewed validation.** Bruehl, Doherty, Streblyanska and Villarroel, *Scientific Reports*, published 10 August 2026: a machine-learning classifier trained on 250 expert-labelled plate pairs taken thirty minutes apart (real versus plate defect; out-of-fold AUC 0.81, sensitivity and specificity 0.71) is deployed across 107,875 previously identified transients. After controlling for ML-identified artefacts, transient counts are significantly elevated within ±1 day of a nuclear test (*p* = 0.024), most strongly among the transients the model rates most likely to be real (*p* < 0.0001), and the Earth's-shadow deficit is significant (*p* < 0.001) and largest in that same high-probability subset (*p* = 0.003).
- **Independent replication.** Doherty (arXiv:2604.00056, 31 March 2026) reproduces the nuclear-window association (relative risk 1.45) with negative binomial regression controlling for precipitation, lunar illumination and cloud cover, confirms temporal specificity by permutation test, and reproduces the shadow deficit, with the note that the uniform-sky expectation is the wrong baseline and the plate-specific one is more conservative.
- **Credentialed critique.** Watters, Dominé, Little, Pratt, Knuth and Szenher (arXiv:2601.21946, January 2026, revised February 2026) argue that the assumption of a spatially uniform-random background is false for the datasets used, that with a proper null distribution **no statistically significant shadow deficit remains**, that a third of the "linear cluster" features are not confidently distinguished from catalogue stars, that the reported nuclear correlation becomes insignificant once normalised by the number of observing days and is almost entirely determined by the Palomar telescope's observing schedule, and that the program contains a circular argument in which the consequences of the hypothesis are used to justify confidence in the measurements. Villarroel et al. reply at arXiv:2602.15171; a later paper by the same group (arXiv:2609.09461, 8 September 2026) supplies the Earth-projected hotspots discussed on the podcast, and an independent ML-scored alignment analysis (arXiv:2605.01190) reports linear alignments projecting to constant geographic longitudes.

**Established fact:** all of the above papers, their dates and the interview exist and are publicly readable; the ML validation is peer-reviewed, while the replication, the critique, the reply, the hotspot paper and the alignment paper are preprints of varying age. The underlying dataset is the VASCO/POSS-I transient catalogue of 107,875 candidates, drawn from digitised plates of a survey conducted 1949–1957.

**Evidence status:** **scientific evidence in the sense of a live, falsifiable, actively contested research programme** — and explicitly **not** evidence of NHI, of non-human surveillance, of a recovery, or of psionic contact. The hotspots are a *projection onto the ground of an assumed orbit*; the surveillance reading is the authors' own interpretation, offered as such; the critique's methodological objections are substantive and are not resolved in the material read this run. What is new for this watch is the *structure*: a seventy-year-old archive anomaly now carries published statistics, an independent replication, a named rebuttal with a reply, and a media channel — the whole evidence ladder present at once, with the strongest rung (independent confirmation by a second instrument, and a physical mechanism that survives the critique) still missing. Two honest failure modes remain live: plate-defect and survey-schedule systematics on the one side, and a real but unexplained atmospheric or orbital phenomenon on the other.
