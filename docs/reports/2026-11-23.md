#### *Animal Spiral* — Luis Othoniel Rosa, tr. Katie Marya (novel; Charco Press, 23 June 2026, 448 pp., £12.99; Spanish and English published simultaneously) — late catch, judged on publisher copy, a published extract and a full review

**What it is.** Two middle-aged streamer twins in Bayamón, Puerto Rico become the first human beings to connect, sharing consciousness across 34 translucent cables, and something is born between them that the book calls the Animal: "an intracerebral force" that grows to take in anthills of synaptically entwined bodies, a floating library kitchen redolent of rice and beans above the Mississippi, and a transhuman compound in a future Cuba. The novel is narrated throughout by that collective — but a collective "within which individuals exist", who call themselves *we someones* and say of themselves: "We don't have access to the other's thoughts, but we do learn to think as they think." It runs across four centuries: a revolution in San Juan, the birth of the Animal in the 2030s (the *Locus* review dates the connection to 2032), a revolution described as complete by 2042, then the Animal's death and its five post-human children — Squash (humanoid), Calima (beetles), Yemayá (eels), Coatlicue (serpents), Juracán (anthropomorphic birds) — who have millions of bodies and all the world's intelligence and, in the publisher's words, "only want to no longer be alone". The author calls it a collaborative project with thirteen other contributors, edited "in an assembly" in weekly pandemic sessions.

**Release state.** **Released** 23 June 2026 in the UK and US simultaneously with the Spanish edition, print and ebook; composed in Spanish, translated by Katie Marya; 448 pp.; ISBN 9781917260329 (print), 9781917260336 (ebook). Reviewed at length in *Locus* by Niall Harrison (23 June 2026) and listed in *Speculative Fiction in Translation*'s 2026 roll. Not previously covered in this lane.

**The specific affinity, in one disputable sentence.** The novel's claim is that a collective mind is made of persons who remain persons — connection gives shared emotion and a learned way of thinking, never access to another's thoughts — so the Noösphere is a practice of translation rather than a fusion, and "we someones" is a grammatical achievement rather than a dissolution.

**What NoöPunk could take, and where it diverges.** Five things.

- **"We someones" as a rule, not a mood.** The sentence that carries it is short: "We don't have access to the other's thoughts, but we do learn to think as they think." NoöPunk's psi and assemblage scenes have no such clause, and without it a merged mind is either a hive or a metaphor. As a table rule it is nearly free: when two characters share a channel, emotional state is public and propositional content is not, and any claim to know what the other is thinking is a claim, not a perception.
- **Affective meteorology.** After the shutdown, in the published extract, "we all have telempathic abilities … we all feel the strong emotions of each one of our 5 billion bodies. A cyclone of emotions. Some of we become meteorologists of these affective climates. We forecast, in detail, the emotional climate of the next few days based on an inexact science." That is a Noöspheric institution of a type the setting does not yet have: not a secret agency and not a priesthood, but a public forecasting service whose object is the mood of the multitude and whose method is openly inexact.
- **The library kitchens.** Institutions where "stories can be exchanged for food" — a commons infrastructure with a rule a campaign can use in one line, and the novel's answer to the question of what a post-scarcity archive is actually for.
- **A structure that models two kinds of time.** Chapters grow in length roughly along the Fibonacci sequence, and the reader is offered two orders — the printed one and a chronological one — because "time after connecting … flows in cycles and spirals". NoöPunk's chronology is deliberately unfixed; this is a worked example of making that unfixity an experience rather than a disclaimer.
- **An ethic that refuses its own utopia.** The review's summary is the useful part: the novel "carefully refuses the idea that the Animal's society, for all its utopian work, is in itself utopia", insists that history never ends, and has the Animal conclude that "the universe could do without us". That is the corrective NoöPunk's wonder needs — a wider mind that is real, good at some things, and not the end of history.

Divergence: the psi is engineered, not fundamental. It arrives as the side effect of 34 cables and a "shutdown", which makes the novel a story about hardware and politics rather than about mind in the physics. There is no NHI, no disclosure and no state legibility regime; the political question is decolonial revolution in Puerto Rico and the Caribbean, not who registers a body. And the collective, however qualified, is greeted with joy — the book "wants you to love the future" — where NoöPunk's contact layer is built to cost something.

**Established fact:** author, translator, publisher, simultaneous publication, date, page count, price, both ISBNs; the Bayamón twins and the 34 cables; the Animal as "intracerebral force"; the anthills, the floating library kitchen and the Cuban compound; "we someones" and the no-access-to-thoughts sentence; the 2042 revolution and the 2068 Mona Island frame; the five post-human siblings and their body-counts; the Fibonacci chapter lengths and the two reading orders; the library kitchens, the clades ("viral transmission identities"), the Coders and the Pumpkin, the Growers, the Cooks, the Historians, the Runners and the Archaeologist; the telempathy extract and the affective meteorologists; the thirteen collaborators and the "assembly" editing — the last three as publisher copy, published extract and review, not as text read end to end. **Not** established here: the prose at length, the novel's own handling of the shutdown, or anything about the Spanish original beyond the fact that it exists and was composed first.

**Evidence status:** publisher, extract and review level; fiction. Nothing here documents collective consciousness, telempathy, or a real post-human transition. A novel that narrates a "we" well establishes only that a "we" can be narrated.

#### *Dead New World* — Lin Darrow (novel; Podium Publishing, 17 November 2026, 336 pp., $19.99; *Flicker City Phantoms* book 1) — released this week, judged on publisher copy and two trade reviews

**What it is.** In 3025 the Flicker/Helix company "cured" death with holographic hardware, and the resurrected — flickers — are "secondary citizens, lacking purpose and agency". Hildy Doval is dead and still unemployed: the least-requested flicker-ghost at Carlotta Sun's Purgatory House for Spectral Services, competing daily for gigs against a musician who never finished his masterpiece and a silent-film extra whose one surviving shot made her famous after death. A headless ghost called the Graverobber has begun stepping out of holographic billboards and taking the resurrected off the street, a fellow boarder goes missing, and there are two months until an election that will decide the future of all specters: one candidate wants them isolated in remote ghost towns, the other wants them integrated. *Library Journal* adds the detail that matters: there are some 10,000 flickers, and they are "caged by their need for electricity to maintain their forms, chained to their 'owners,' bound up in restrictive laws", and treated as scapegoats in the middle of a divisive election.

**Release state.** **Released** 17 November 2026 in paperback, ebook and audiobook; first of a duology; ISBN 9798347040063. Trade reviews: *Library Journal* (Marlene Harris, 1 September 2026) and *Kirkus* ("'Trippy' doesn't begin to describe this wild yarn"). Marketed as cozy cyber-noir; one retailer listing shelves it under cyberpunk.

**The specific affinity, in one disputable sentence.** The book's premise is that the uploaded dead are a labouring class before they are a metaphysical puzzle — resurrection is a service sold by a company, the resurrected rent their own continuance from an electricity supply and a contract, and whether a copy is a person is settled not by philosophy but by an election.

**What NoöPunk could take, and where it diverges.** Four things.

- **Dependence as the ownership clause.** "Caged by their need for electricity" is the cleanest statement of the augmentation question this lane has found in fiction: the body is not owned by patent but by the utility that keeps it instantiated, and the legal tie ("chained to their owners") follows the cable. NoöPunk prices continuity in money or in sanity; this prices it in power draw, which is both a resource mechanic and a politics.
- **The election clock.** Two candidates, two policies — isolation in ghost towns or integration — and a countdown measured in months. That is a political timer rather than a combat clock: the party's actions change who is eligible to vote on whether they exist.
- **The spectral services house.** A boarding house that brokers gigs for the dead is an institution of exactly the kind a NoöPunk city needs — neither a corporation nor a ministry, but a vendor with a client list, and therefore a faction with interests of its own.
- **The scapegoat mechanism.** *Library Journal*'s summary names the thing most upload fiction skips: the resurrected are useful as a group to resent. Prejudice as an electoral resource is a better antagonist than a rogue AI.

Divergence: personhood is presumed. The novel's question is civil rights, not whether the copy is the person, so it never touches the identity-continuity problem NoöPunk's resleeving mechanics have to answer. It is also cozy by design, set in 3025 with no psi, no Noösphere and no contact, and *Library Journal* notes that the tech itself is "a bit of handwavium" — the substrate is a premise, not an argument.

**Established fact:** author, publisher, date, page count, price, ISBN, duology position, formats; the Flicker/Helix premise and the phrase "secondary citizens"; Hildy Doval, Carlotta Sun's Purgatory House for Spectral Services, Eyvind and Anjali; the Graverobber and the billboards; the two-month election and the two candidate policies; the 10,000 flickers, the electricity dependence, the "owners", the restrictive laws and the scapegoating (all from *Library Journal*); the *Kirkus* line. **Not** established: the prose, the book's actual treatment of continuity, or how the election resolves.

**Evidence status:** publisher and trade-review level; fiction. Nothing here is evidence about uploading, holographic substrates, machine persons or AI rights. A fictional electricity bill is not a finding about substrate dependence; it is a design argument about where to put the cost.

#### *Waiting on a Friend* — Natalie Adler (novel; Hogarth, 26 May 2026, 288 pp., $28; Quercus in the UK) — late catch, judged on two independent reviews

**What it is.** East Village, summer 1984. Renata, a young dyke-about-town, sees ghosts, and has been seeing more of them as her friends die of the disease that has just been named. Her best friend Mark dies and does not come back, which is the novel's engine: the one dead person she wants is the one who will not appear. Against that, a "mysterious, police-like force" begins clearing ghosts out of the neighbourhood — the *Los Angeles Review of Books* calls them "ghost cops", "agents of the sterilizing force of gentrification intent on erasing the ghosts *and* the queer past they represent". *Strange Horizons* puts the same fact in one clause: the agency clears ghosts from gentrified buildings "to make them more palatable for residents who have money".

**Release state.** **Released** 26 May 2026 (Hogarth US; Quercus UK, ISBN 9781529439052); 288 pp.; a debut; named to *Time*'s 100 Must-Read Books of 2026. Reviewed in *Strange Horizons* on 20 November 2026, which is what brought it into this lane.

**The specific affinity, in one disputable sentence.** The novel treats mediumship as an ordinary faculty inside an ordinary property market — the dead are residents, and their removal is a real-estate decision — so the political question about the anomalous is not whether it is real but who is paying to have it cleared.

**What NoöPunk could take, and where it diverges.** Three things.

- **The vendor, not the agency.** NoöPunk's psi institutions are currently secret programmes and contact cults. A remediation contractor hired by landlords is the third type, and the most cyberpunk of the three: the anomaly is not classified, it is subcontracted, and the bill goes to whoever owns the building.
- **"You can tell what used to be there."** Renata's account of seeing ghosts — buildings that have disappeared, "low-rise outlines against the brick, hovering over construction sites" — is a perception rule for a psi character in a gentrifying city: they see the layer under the current build. It costs one sentence on a character sheet and does more world-building than a page of setting text.
- **The channel that will not open.** The plot is driven by a contact that fails: the ghost who does not return. NoöPunk's contact scenes assume the phenomenon arrives; this is the version where the loved dead decline, and the story is what the living build around the silence.

Divergence: the world's rules never break. Ghosts are ambient from page one, the novel is magical realism rather than paradigm break, and nothing in it is discovered, contested or institutionalised beyond one cleaning company. There is no cyberpunk, no Noösphere and no psi theory; 1984 is the setting, not a timeline beat. Reviewed as literary fiction with a supernatural twist, it clears this lane narrowly and should not be cited as fiction *for* panpsychism.

**Established fact:** author, publishers, date, page count, price, both ISBNs, debut status and the *Time* listing; Renata's faculty, Mark's death and non-appearance, the clearing agency and the gentrification frame, the *LARB* "ghost cops" characterisation, the *Strange Horizons* formulation, Renata's account of seeing what used to be there. **Not** established: the prose, the agency's name or internal logic, or the novel's resolution.

**Evidence status:** review level; fiction. Two independent reviews and a magazine review agree on the premise; none of that is evidence about mediumship, survival, or the dead. It is evidence that a mainstream literary novel in 2026 can carry a psi premise without arguing for it, which is a cultural datum of exactly one bit.
